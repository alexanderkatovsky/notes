# Goodhart's Law in Reinforcement Learning — Study Notes

Notes from a walkthrough of Karwowski et al., **“Goodhart’s Law in Reinforcement Learning.”**

The central idea is to reinterpret RL geometrically: an MDP defines a constrained space of achievable behaviours, while a reward function defines a direction in that space. Increasingly optimizing an imperfect proxy reward can eventually move the agent away from the true objective.

## 1. The basic MDP

An MDP can be written as

$$
\langle S,A,\tau,\mu,R,\gamma\rangle,
$$

where:

- $S$ = states
- $A$ = actions
- $\tau$ = transition dynamics
- $\mu$ = initial-state distribution
- $R$ = reward function
- $\gamma\in[0,1]$ = discount factor

A trajectory is

$$
\xi=(s_0,a_0,s_1,a_1,\ldots).
$$

The discounted return is

$$
G(\xi)=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^tR(s_t,a_t),
$$

and the expected return of policy $\pi$ is

$$
J_R(\pi)=\mathbb E_{\xi\sim\pi}[G(\xi)].
$$

### Why $\gamma$?

For infinite-horizon problems, $\gamma<1$ makes the discounted sum well behaved and gives greater weight to earlier rewards.

For finite-horizon problems, $\gamma=1$ can be perfectly reasonable. For example, in a game where only the eventual outcome matters, the exact time at which the reward arrives may not matter.

## 2. Occupancy measures

The key change of viewpoint is to represent a policy by the distribution of state-action pairs it induces.

An occupancy measure has the form

$$
\eta^\pi(s,a).
$$

Intuitively, it represents the expected discounted amount of time that policy $\pi$ spends taking action $a$ in state $s$.

A common normalized definition is

$$
\eta^\pi(s,a)
=
(1-\gamma)
\mathbb E_\pi
\left[
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}
\gamma^t
\mathbf 1\{s_t=s,a_t=a\}
\right].
$$

The exact normalization convention matters for factors of $(1-\gamma)$, but the geometric idea is the same: each policy becomes a vector describing how much occupancy it puts on every state-action pair.

Since there is one number for every $(s,a)$ pair,

$$
\eta^\pi\in\mathbb R^{|S||A|}.
$$

So instead of thinking primarily about

$$
\pi(a\mid s),
$$

we can think of the policy as a point $\eta^\pi$ in a high-dimensional vector space.

## 3. The occupancy polytope $\Omega$

Not every vector in $\mathbb R^{|S||A|}$ can be an occupancy measure. The transition dynamics $\tau$ constrain which occupancy measures are physically achievable.

Define

$$
\Omega=\{\eta^\pi:\pi\in\Pi\}.
$$

This is the set of all occupancy measures achievable by policies.

The important geometric result is

$$
\boxed{\Omega=\operatorname{conv}\{\eta^\pi:\pi\in\Pi_0\}}
$$

where $\Pi_0$ is the set of deterministic policies.

In words: the occupancy measures of stochastic policies form the convex hull of the occupancy measures of deterministic policies.

Therefore $\Omega$ is a **convex polytope**.

A polytope is essentially the higher-dimensional analogue of a polygon: a bounded convex region generated by finitely many points.

The affine dimension of $\Omega$ is

$$
|S|(|A|-1).
$$

However, the raw occupancy vectors still live in the ambient space

$$
\mathbb R^{|S||A|}.
$$

So it is useful to distinguish

$$
\eta,R\in\mathbb R^{|S||A|}
$$

from

$$
\Omega\subset\mathbb R^{|S||A|},
$$

where $\Omega$ lies in a lower-dimensional affine subspace.

## 4. Why deterministic policies appear at the vertices

A linear function maximized over a convex polytope attains its maximum at at least one vertex.

Therefore ordinary reward maximization has an optimal deterministic policy (ignoring degeneracies where multiple policies can be equally optimal).

The vertices correspond to occupancy measures generated by deterministic policies, while points inside the polytope correspond to achievable stochastic policies.

## 5. Reward as a linear functional

A reward assigns one scalar to each state-action pair, so it can be represented as a vector

$$
R\in\mathbb R^{|S||A|}.
$$

The key observation is that expected reward can be written as an inner product:

$$
J_R(\pi)=\eta^\pi\cdot R
$$

(up to the normalization convention used for $\eta$).

Define the linear functional

$$
L_R(\eta)=\eta\cdot R.
$$

Then ordinary RL becomes

$$
\boxed{\max_{\eta\in\Omega}\eta\cdot R.}
$$

This is a linear-programming problem over the occupancy polytope.

The conceptual decomposition is:

$$
\text{MDP dynamics}\longrightarrow\Omega
$$

and

$$
\text{reward}\longrightarrow L_R(\eta)=\eta\cdot R.
$$

So the dynamics determine **which behaviours are feasible**, while the reward determines **which feasible behaviour is preferred**.

## 6. Geometric interpretation of the reward

Think of $R$ as a direction in the ambient vector space.

The level sets of

$$
L_R(\eta)=\eta\cdot R
$$

are hyperplanes perpendicular to $R$.

Maximizing reward means moving such a hyperplane in the direction of increasing reward until it touches $\Omega$.

Because $\Omega$ is a polytope, an optimum occurs at a boundary point, and generically at a vertex.

So the whole RL optimization problem becomes:

$$
\boxed{\text{reward direction}+\text{feasible occupancy polytope}=\text{RL optimization}}
$$

## 7. The role of $M_\tau$

The occupancy polytope lies in a lower-dimensional affine subspace of the ambient space. Consequently, not every component of $R$ is relevant when comparing feasible policies.

The paper introduces $M_\tau$ as the projection associated with the relevant occupancy-space geometry.

Conceptually:

$$
R\longrightarrow M_\tau R.
$$

The relevant reward direction is therefore the projected vector $M_\tau R$.

This leads to the paper's definition of the angle between two reward functions:

$$
\arg(R_0,R_1)=\angle(M_\tau R_0,M_\tau R_1).
$$

Thus the paper is not simply comparing the raw reward vectors in $\mathbb R^{|S||A|}$; it is comparing their directions after taking account of the MDP's feasible occupancy geometry.

## 8. True reward versus proxy reward

Now introduce two rewards:

- $R_0$: the **true reward**
- $R_1$: the **proxy reward**

The interesting failure mode is

$$
J_{R_1}(\pi)\quad\text{continues increasing}
$$

while

$$
J_{R_0}(\pi)\quad\text{eventually decreases}.
$$

In other words:

$$
\boxed{\text{better proxy performance}\not\Rightarrow\text{better true performance}}
$$

when optimization becomes sufficiently strong.

## 9. Optimization pressure

Optimization pressure is not necessarily a particular algorithm or hyperparameter. It is an abstraction for how far or how aggressively we optimize the proxy objective.

The paper deliberately abstracts away from the concrete optimization algorithm and studies movement through occupancy space toward higher proxy reward.

A schematic trajectory is

$$
\eta^{\pi_0}\rightarrow\eta^{\pi_1}\rightarrow\eta^{\pi_2}\rightarrow\cdots
$$

with

$$
J_{R_1}(\pi_0)<J_{R_1}(\pi_1)<J_{R_1}(\pi_2)<\cdots.
$$

Increasing optimization pressure means pushing farther along this process.

## 10. What does optimization pressure mean for DQN?

Suppose the proxy reward is what the DQN is actually trained to maximize.

Then one natural practical proxy for increasing optimization pressure is simply:

$$
\boxed{\text{more training steps}\approx\text{more opportunity to optimize the proxy}}
$$

Other DQN choices can influence the optimization process:

- learning rate
- number of gradient updates
- network capacity
- exploration schedule
- replay-buffer configuration
- target-network updates
- regularization

But these should not be identified one-to-one with the paper's abstract notion of optimization pressure.

The cleaner interpretation is:

> **DQN determines how you move through policy/occupancy space; optimization pressure describes how far you push the proxy optimization.**

## 11. Goodharting versus neural-network overfitting

An observation like

$$
\text{proxy score}\uparrow\qquad\text{true score}\downarrow
$$

after extended training might initially look like neural-network overfitting.

But there is another explanation:

> The network is successfully doing what it was asked to do: continuing to optimize the proxy. The problem is that the proxy is imperfect.

This distinction matters because the phenomenon does not fundamentally require a neural network.

Conceptually, the same issue could arise with tabular Q-learning, policy iteration, evolutionary search, or another policy optimizer.

The neural network is one implementation mechanism for traversing the space of policies. The Goodhart phenomenon is about what can happen when we continue pushing toward high proxy reward.

## 12. Entropy regularization

The paper also considers causal entropy regularization.

The regularized objective has the form

$$
\tilde R(s,a)=R(s,a)+\alpha H(\pi(\cdot\mid s)),
$$

where

$$
H(\pi(\cdot\mid s))=-\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\log\pi(a\mid s).
$$

The important point is that entropy is nonlinear in the policy.

Therefore, unlike

$$
\max_{\eta\in\Omega}\eta\cdot R,
$$

the entropy-regularized problem is **not simply a linear program over $\Omega$**.

The entropy term encourages stochastic policies.

## 13. What does $\alpha$ do?

If

$$
\alpha=0,
$$

there is no entropy regularization:

$$
\tilde R=R.
$$

So we recover ordinary reward maximization:

$$
\max_{\eta\in\Omega}\eta\cdot R.
$$

This is the linear-programming case, and an optimal policy can be deterministic.

As $\alpha$ increases, entropy becomes increasingly important, encouraging stochasticity.

In the regime where entropy dominates reward, the policy approaches the maximum-entropy policy. If all available actions are treated symmetrically, this is uniform:

$$
\pi(a\mid s)=\frac{1}{|A(s)|}.
$$

So conceptually:

$$
\boxed{\text{reward dominated}\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad\text{entropy dominated}}
$$

corresponds roughly to

$$
\boxed{\text{deterministic reward optimization}\quad\longleftrightarrow\quad\text{maximally stochastic policy}.}
$$

Do not confuse this entropy coefficient $\alpha$ with the paper's broader conceptual notion of optimization pressure. They refer to different ideas.

## 14. The complete geometric picture

The core conceptual transformation is

$$
\boxed{\text{MDP}\rightarrow\text{occupancy polytope }\Omega\rightarrow\text{reward vector }R\rightarrow\text{linear objective }\eta\cdot R}
$$

Now introduce

$$
R_0=\text{true objective}
$$

and

$$
R_1=\text{proxy objective}.
$$

Increasing optimization pressure means pushing farther toward high $R_1$ within $\Omega$.

If the two objectives are sufficiently misaligned, we can get

$$
J_{R_1}(\pi_t)\uparrow
$$

while

$$
J_{R_0}(\pi_t)
$$

first increases and then decreases.

That is the geometric form of Goodhart's law.

## 15. The mental model

> **The MDP gives you a constrained geometric space of achievable behaviours. The reward is a direction telling you which behaviours to prefer. Goodharting happens when you push increasingly hard in the proxy direction and eventually move into regions where the proxy and true objective come apart.**

Or, more compactly:

$$
\boxed{\text{feasible behaviours}+\text{proxy direction}+\text{increasing optimization}\Rightarrow\text{possible Goodharting}}
$$

The neural network, optimizer, and training algorithm determine **how** you traverse this space in a concrete implementation. The geometric argument is intended to explain the more general phenomenon.
